Indie Game Engine Guide: Godot vs Unity vs Construct 3

Guide · Back to guides

"Which engine should I use?" is the first question most new game developers ask, and there's no single right answer. The best fit depends on the genre you're making, your team size, how much programming experience you have, and your monetization plans. This article compares the three engines indie developers reach for most often - Godot, Unity, and Construct 3 - on the criteria that actually matter once you start building. For a quick rundown of three more engines (Unreal Engine, GameMaker, RPG Maker), see Game-Making Tools.

What actually matters when choosing

People often focus on raw performance, but these five factors tend to matter more in practice.

  • Learning curve - if you have no coding background, no-code/low-code support can be the difference between finishing your first game and not.
  • Monetization structure - whether the engine is free or takes a revenue-based royalty directly affects a small team's budget.
  • 2D focus - engines built heavily around 3D can feel unnecessarily heavy and complex for a purely 2D game.
  • Community and resources - how easily you can find an answer when you're stuck has a huge effect on how hard the engine feels day to day.
  • Target platforms - check in advance that the engine supports your intended platforms (PC, mobile, web, console) well.

Godot - free, open source, and strong for 2D

Godot is completely free and open source, with no royalties or revenue share on commercial projects. Its scripting language, GDScript, has Python-like syntax that's relatively quick to pick up for programming beginners. Its 2D workflow - tilemaps, animation trees, 2D physics - is mature out of the box, which pairs well with pixel-art-style indie games. The trade-off is that its 3D tooling and large-project asset pipeline are less mature than Unity's or Unreal's, and its tutorial and plugin ecosystem is still smaller than the commercial engines'.

Unity - the widest ecosystem, built on C#

Unity is one of the most widely used commercial engines in the world, supporting both 2D and 3D with C# scripting. Its massive asset store and overwhelming volume of tutorials and Q&A content mean you can usually search your way out of a problem. Individuals and small teams can start on the free plan, moving to a paid plan once revenue passes a set threshold. The trade-off is that C# has a slightly steeper entry curve than GDScript for absolute beginners, and build times and configuration complexity tend to grow along with the project.

Construct 3 - no code, right in your browser

Construct 3 is a no-code engine where you build logic by dragging and dropping events and actions instead of writing code. It runs entirely in the browser, so there's no install step at all, and someone with zero programming experience can have a playable prototype within a few days. It's optimized for 2D games, especially casual, puzzle, and arcade genres. On the other hand, it's a poor fit for complex custom logic that needs fine-grained code or for 3D games, and it runs on a subscription pricing model worth factoring in.

Recommendation by situation

  • Making a 2D pixel-art game with no budget → Godot. No royalties, and the 2D workflow is ready from day one.
  • Want to search your way out of problems, and might expand into 3D later → Unity. It has the largest resource base and community by far.
  • No coding experience and want a playable prototype fast → Construct 3. Start right in the browser, no install needed.

All three engines are free (or free to trial), so rather than choosing from an article alone, it's worth following a short official tutorial in each one and picking whichever feels right in your hands.
