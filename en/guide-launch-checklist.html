First Indie Game Launch Checklist (itch.io / Steam)

Guide · Back to guides

Finishing a game doesn't mean you're ready to hit publish. A store page, screenshots, and pricing require a different kind of preparation than development itself, and skipping that step can leave even a genuinely good game unnoticed. This guide walks through what to check before launching your first game on itch.io or Steam.

Pick a platform first: itch.io vs Steam

itch.io is free and instant to publish on with no review process, making it a good fit for a first game or a game jam entry you want out in the world quickly. Pricing is entirely up to you, including "pay what you want." Steam, on the other hand, has a listing fee and requires a review process and store page prep time, but reaches an incomparably larger audience. Many first-time developers launch on itch.io to gauge reaction before preparing a Steam release.

Store page assets to prepare

  • 1. Screenshots4-6 images of actual gameplay. Real gameplay moments convert better than a static title screen.
  • 2. A short pitchThe genre and core hook should be clear within the first sentence or two - a reader should know "what kind of game this is" immediately.
  • 3. A trailer or GIFA short gameplay clip or GIF noticeably increases time-on-page and conversion compared to screenshots alone.
  • 4. System requirements and controlsNote whether it's keyboard/mouse or controller-supported, and list minimum specs up front.

Final checks before launch

  • Has the build actually been run on someone else's machine (ideally more than one spec)?
  • Is there a channel ready for bug reports and feedback (Discord, email, comments)?
  • If you've set a price, can you justify it against similarly scoped games?
  • Have you re-checked that every asset's license permits commercial distribution? See the free assets and licensing guide for details.

What to watch right after launch

The first few days after release are when most of the initial reaction and bug reports arrive. Be ready to ship a quick patch if a critical bug turns up, and prioritize any feedback that keeps repeating across players. For a small game especially, word of mouth from those first few players has an outsized effect on everything that follows.
